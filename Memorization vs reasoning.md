Semantic memorization vs reasoning 

Transformers in LLMs were originally used for language generation.

But with the advent of CoT they are increasingly used for reasoning.

I've been wondering how effective are transformers in "pure" reasoning?

---------------------------------

Grokking demonstrated that transformers can generalize to unseen data.

But considering that they require ~100x the amount of training to "reason" than to "memorize", it is still debatable how appropriate they are for reasoning?

There's also ample work that shows that transformers can't generalize to everything. Particularly, in the space of length generalization which impacts the ability of transformers to compose: combine two previously learned tasks.

Programatically, this is reflected in the inability to use loops. Which ultimately I think is grounded in the traditional feed-forward architecture of neural networks. While recurrent transformers are promising they are still niche, and inefficient.

---------------------------------

# How to test a transformer's ability to "reason"?

I started working with labyrinths. Unfortunately they suffer from a "2D bias" where convolution transformers drastically outperform.

Diving into kimi linear, I noticed synthetic datasets tested language modeling capabilities - among which retrieval - but not reasoning.

This is relevant because linear attention attempts to reconstruct full attention. But I would argue there is redundant information in context that is not required for the next reasoning step - but maybe later. In theory, linear attention could outperform full attention if instead of reconstructing "finds" the most relevant information.


I finally settled on testing finding the shortest path in graphs. Which is a generalization of labyrinths, tokens do not posses any "semantic" bias, and has applications to CoT. 
  
---------------------------------

# Testing reasoning on graphs

There are two flavors of CoT. The first executes a memorized plan across a long sequence of steps (solving a math equation). 

In the second, CoT is part of generating the plan - proving a theorem. Nowhere is this more evident than on DeepSeek Math Prover V2.

The idea was to provide a "suboptimal" trace, and ask the transform to find the optimal path.

First, I started with paths generated by DFS. But the transformers quickly learned a heuristic.

Later, I attempted a random walk. The path was relatively simple mostly with back and forths between 2 nodes. But even there I noticed transformers started to struggle with achieving 100% accuracy.

Now, I'm testing with dense graphs, and finally see transformers struggling. You can even see that validation loss is increasing. The exact behavior pre-grokking. 

---------------------------------

# How do transformers plan?

In the landmark circuits analysis by Anthorpic. They realize that no transformers (pure embeddings) equals bigram likelihood maximization.

A first layer of transformers, enabled skip trigrams. Where the prediction was informed by the most "informative" previous token in the sequence.

With two layers and beyond, transformers used "subspaces" to communicate between layers. Drastically complicating interpretability.

The question becomes how good transformers are to produce attention, when memorization is of little value? This has applications to coding agents, retrieval (deep research), multi-agent systems, scientific discovery and more!

I suspect the answers are not solely on transformers, but on test-time-training (TTT).

---------------------------------

# What constitutes a good plan?

Preliminary results on the "easy" datasets, show two metrics that correlate with a good plan:

1. Attention entropy: the more attention is distributed across input tokens the more errors - the problem is more complex.

2. Classification certainty when making a prediction in the final layer.

This opens applications to reasoning verification.  

On an analysis of what constitutes challenging predictions steps. Unsurprisingly, it was sequence length.

The results are preliminary, but I have to ask: what if better attention is possible (test-trained)? What if transformers are not the cornerstone for reasoning?

There is an implicit tension between the ability to memorize in which transformers excel, and the ability to reason that requires "thinking from first principles". Leaving behind what you think you already know, and generalizing with minimal data!
